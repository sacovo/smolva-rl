\begin{abstract}
Critic-guided rollout post-training, introduced by RECAP and demonstrated with the large
$\pi^{*}_{0.6}$ vision-language-action (VLA) model, improves a policy by training on its own
critic-scored rollouts. We study whether this recipe transfers to SmolVLA, a compact
flow-matching VLA whose size makes it a candidate for edge and mobile robots, using the LIBERO
benchmark. In contrast to RECAP, our reinforcement phase uses no human intervention: rollouts
are collected autonomously and labeled by a learned critic alone. The recipe transfers, with one qualification: training on rollout data alone improves the
two short-horizon suites but degrades the long-horizon one. Pre-training with knowledge
insulation and advantage conditioning gains $\approx$$10$ points per suite over a plain
SmolVLA baseline trained without either; co-training the rollout data with expert
demonstration batches (mixing ratio $0.5$) removes the long-horizon regression and is the
best configuration, reaching $67.8\%$ average success across three suites, compared to
$62.9\%$ for the base policy and $52.4\%$ for the plain baseline. One practical caveat is the advantage-labeling threshold: if it is set
aggressively, actions that are necessary for success are labeled negative and the policy
degrades; a relaxed threshold avoids this. Classifier-free guidance at inference contributes
little in our setting ($\sim$1 point); the gains come from the advantage-conditioned training
itself. Finally, one-step SnapFlow distillation removes the
iterative flow-matching solve at inference, cutting action-chunk latency $3.6\times$ on an
NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano.
These results indicate that rollout-based post-training, so far demonstrated on
large VLAs, is also applicable to compact models suited to resource-constrained robots.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Vision-language-action (VLA) policies map camera images, proprioceptive robot state, and a
language instruction to robot actions. The strongest current VLAs are large models: the
$\pi^{*}_{0.6}$ policy underlying RECAP~\cite{b_recap} couples a $4$B-parameter Gemma~3
backbone with an $860$M-parameter action expert, and the reference inference stack for this
model family calls for a GPU with more than $8$\,GB of memory~\cite{b_openpi}. The documented
example, an RTX~4090, has a $450$\,W board power rating, and the stack often runs the policy
off-robot entirely, streaming actions to the robot over a network. A battery-powered mobile robot offers a
small fraction of that budget: a representative onboard computer such as the NVIDIA Jetson
Orin Nano provides, within a $7$--$25$\,W envelope, $8$\,GB of unified memory shared with the
operating system~\cite{b_jetson}---too little to hold even the bfloat16 weights of
$\pi^{*}_{0.6}$ ($\approx$$10$\,GB). SmolVLA~\cite{b_smolvla} was proposed as a compact alternative
($450$M parameters) that fits this class of hardware, but it is trained by imitation only.

RECAP~\cite{b_recap} showed that a VLA can be improved past its imitation baseline by
\emph{rollout post-training}: the policy collects rollouts, a learned critic scores them with
per-frame advantages, the advantages are thresholded into positive/negative labels, and the
policy is retrained with the label as a conditioning token so that classifier-free guidance
(CFG)~\cite{b_cfg} can steer it at inference. Those results were obtained on a large model
with human teleoperation corrections mixed into the rollout data. Our goal in this paper is to
apply the RECAP recipe to a small model, where the payoff is most direct: if rollout
post-training works for SmolVLA, its gains become available on edge and mobile robots. For the
same reason we add SnapFlow~\cite{b_snapflow} one-step distillation, which removes the
iterative flow-matching solve so that the resulting policy can act in real time on limited
hardware.

Concretely, we run the pipeline on the LIBERO benchmark~\cite{b_libero} and report the
following:
\begin{itemize}
\item RECAP-style rollout post-training transfers to SmolVLA: the knowledge-insulation
recipe~\cite{b_ki} gains $\approx$$10$ points per suite over plain SmolVLA; rollout-only
training helps the short-horizon suites but degrades the long-horizon one; adding expert
demonstrations to the fine-tuning mix lifts all three suites and gives the strongest policy
(Sec.~\ref{sec:main}).
\item An ablation of the advantage-labeling threshold (Sec.~\ref{sec:dose}): setting it too
aggressively labels necessary actions as negative, which degrades the policy and makes CFG
counterproductive; a relaxed threshold ($\pf{=}0.8$) avoids this. Even then, guidance at
inference adds only about one point---the benefit comes from the advantage-conditioned
training itself.
\item Remaining failures concentrate on long-horizon tasks that chain two pick-and-place
subgoals; co-training helps most on skills that are absent from the policy's own rollouts
(Sec.~\ref{sec:failure}).
\item One-step SnapFlow distillation of the best policy, trading the iterative flow-matching
solve for a single step: measured on a Jetson Orin Nano, chunk generation drops from $922$ to
$255$\,ms (Sec.~\ref{sec:snapflow}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\textbf{Flow-matching VLA policies.} SmolVLA~\cite{b_smolvla} couples a compact
vision-language backbone with a flow-matching~\cite{b_flow} action expert that integrates a
learned velocity field to generate a chunk of actions from images, robot state, and language.
\emph{Knowledge insulation} (KI)~\cite{b_ki} trains the action expert while limiting gradient
flow into the language backbone and instead supervises the backbone with autoregressive action
targets from the FAST tokenizer~\cite{b_fast}, preserving pretrained semantics.

\textbf{Critic-guided rollout post-training.} RECAP~\cite{b_recap} trains a distributional
critic on policy rollouts, computes $N$-step temporal-difference advantages, and retrains the
policy with advantage tokens so CFG can steer it toward high-advantage behavior. In RECAP the
rollout data additionally contains corrective human teleoperation. Other work fine-tunes VLAs
with online policy-gradient RL~\cite{b_irevla,b_simplevlarl} or trajectory-level preference
alignment~\cite{b_grape}; we study specifically the critic-relabeling recipe, which requires
neither reward shaping nor online policy-gradient infrastructure.

\textbf{Classifier-free guidance.} CFG~\cite{b_cfg} extrapolates between a conditional and an
unconditional generator. Here the conditional uses a positive-advantage token and the
unconditional drops it; guidance weight $w{=}0$ recovers the unconditional policy and $w{=}1$
the positive conditional.

\textbf{One-step distillation.} SnapFlow~\cite{b_snapflow} distills the multi-step
flow-matching expert into a single target-time-conditioned jump, removing the iterative ODE
solve at inference. We use it to measure the accuracy--latency trade-off of the best policy.

\subsection{Critic and advantage-conditioned pre-training}\label{sec:pretrain}
The pipeline starts with the critic, not the policy. A distributional (C51)~\cite{b_c51}
critic is trained
on the LIBERO expert dataset to predict the remaining time to episode completion as a
categorical distribution; its expectation is the value $V(s_t)$. Fig.~\ref{fig:critic} shows
the critic's output over episodes: the value rises steadily as the robot makes progress and
temporarily drops back when progress stalls (e.g., a missed grasp), which is what makes
per-frame advantages informative within a single trajectory.

The base policy is then pre-trained ($250$k steps) on the same expert dataset with KI
\emph{and advantage conditioning already in place}: per-frame advantages from the
demonstration-trained critic are thresholded per task into positive/negative tokens that are
prepended to the language prefix, with the token dropped at probability $0.3$ so an
unconditional predictor is learned alongside. The resulting base policy $\pi_{\text{base}}$
therefore enters fine-tuning already interpreting the advantage tokens, unconditional
predictor included.

normalized time-to-completion ($-1$ start, $0$ done). The value rises steadily as the robot

\subsection{Rollout collection and critic fine-tuning}
$\pi_{\text{base}}$ collects rollouts on the target suites, yielding $107{,}410$ frames over $600$
episodes ($64.3\%$ success). All rollouts are collected autonomously; unlike RECAP, no human
teleoperation corrections or interventions are used at any point. The critic is then
fine-tuned on this rollout data. This step is necessary: having seen only successful
demonstrations, the pre-training critic is over-optimistic on failed rollouts and assigns
them values close to those of successes. After fine-tuning, per-frame advantages are computed
as $N$-step TD residuals ($N{=}50$) with a terminal penalty for failed episodes, so failed
frames receive strongly negative advantages. These advantages provide the thresholds and
labels for the subsequent policy fine-tuning.

\subsection{Advantage labeling}
For each task, a threshold $\varepsilon_\ell$ is set to the $100(1-\pf)$ percentile of the
advantage distribution over that task's \emph{successful} frames; a frame is labeled positive
iff its advantage exceeds $\varepsilon_\ell$. The target positive fraction \pf{} thus controls
how aggressively frames are labeled negative. We use $\pf{=}0.8$ as the default and ablate
lower values in Sec.~\ref{sec:dose}. We also evaluate an \emph{outcome-only} scheme that
bypasses the critic: every frame of a successful episode is positive, every frame of a failed
episode negative.

\subsection{Advantage-conditioned fine-tuning and CFG}
The policy is fine-tuned from $\pi_{\text{base}}$ on the rollout data, with the rollout labels supplying
the positive/negative token it already knows from pre-training; the same $0.3$ token dropout
keeps the unconditional predictor required for CFG intact. At inference we sweep the guidance weight
$w\in\{0,0.5,1,1.5,2\}$. The guided velocity field is
$v^{\mathrm{cfg}}(x_t)=v_\theta(x_t)+w[v_\theta^{+}(x_t)-v_\theta(x_t)]$, recovering the
unconditional field at $w{=}0$, the positive conditional at $w{=}1$, and extrapolating past
the positive mode for $w{>}1$. The expert integrates the field in ten fixed Euler steps at
inference.


\subsection{Expert co-training}
We optionally mix a fraction (ratio $0.5$) of expert demonstration batches into fine-tuning,
labeling all demonstration frames positive. This supplies known-good behavior for the positive
conditional, including skills that may be absent from the policy's own rollouts.

\subsection{SnapFlow distillation}
Given the best fine-tuned policy, SnapFlow~\cite{b_snapflow} distills the ten-step expert
into a single-step generator conditioned on a target time, initialized so training begins
exactly at the flow-matching teacher.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\textbf{Benchmark.} LIBERO~\cite{b_libero} suites \textsc{spatial}, \textsc{goal}, and
\textsc{long} (LIBERO-10), $10$ tasks each. We do not use \textsc{object}: every policy trained in our
setting, including the base, runs its episodes to timeout without completing the task. Because
this affects all configurations identically, we suspect missing or corrupted \textsc{object}
demonstrations in the pre-training data rather than a property of the methods; either way the
suite cannot separate them. \textbf{Protocol.} Unless noted, every reported number is $50$
episodes per task ($n{=}500$ per suite); we report percent success at the best guidance
weight. All advantage-conditioned methods are swept identically over
$w\in\{0,0.5,1,1.5,2\}$; the no-KI baseline has no conditioning token, so its number is a
single unconditioned evaluation. \textbf{Models.} SmolVLA with a $16$-layer VLM backbone; the base is pre-trained for $250$k
steps on the expert demonstrations with KI, FAST co-training, and advantage conditioning
(Sec.~\ref{sec:pretrain}); fine-tunes run $20$k steps. The no-KI baseline follows the original SmolVLA
recipe: flow-matching gradients from the action expert train the VLM directly (no
insulation), which replaces the FAST AR co-training as the VLM's training signal, and no
advantage conditioning is used---so the comparison to the base measures the KI training
recipe as a whole, not gradient insulation in isolation. Training and evaluation hardware
and run times are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:compute}.

\subsection{Ablation: the advantage-labeling threshold}\label{sec:dose}
Table~\ref{tab:dose} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:dose} sweep the guidance weight for three values of \pf{} on \textsc{spatial}.
At low \pf{} ($0.3$, $0.4$) a large share of the frames \emph{inside successful
trajectories}---the approach and transport motions a task requires---fall below the threshold
and are labeled negative ($26$--$31\%$ of all frames, Table~\ref{tab:labels}). The policy is
then trained to treat necessary actions as behavior to avoid: success drops below the base
even at $w{=}0$ ($67.0$ vs.\ $71.2$ for $\pf{=}0.3$), and guidance amplifies the damage,
since the $v_\theta^{+}\!-\!v_\theta$ direction it extrapolates along then points away from
useful behavior. At
$\pf{=}0.8$ the negative label is reserved for genuinely low-advantage behavior (mostly from
failed episodes), the policy improves over the base, and moderate guidance helps. We therefore
use $\pf{=}0.8$ throughout.

Even in this best case, guidance itself is a minor contributor: it adds $+1.0$ on
\textsc{spatial} ($76.8$ at $w{=}0$ vs.\ $77.8$ at $w{=}0.5$), while nearly all of the gain
over the base is already present at $w{=}0$ ($76.8$ vs.\ $71.2$)---that is, it comes from the
advantage-conditioned fine-tuning on labeled rollouts, not from steering at inference.

\subsection{Where the policies still fail}\label{sec:failure}
Per-task results at $50$ trials carry substantial binomial noise ($\approx\pm14\%$ at $95\%$
confidence), so we restrict the discussion to patterns that are consistent across methods and
clearly outside that noise.

The dominant remaining failure mode is long-horizon tasks that chain \emph{two} pick-and-place
subgoals. All methods stay below $\sim40\%$ on the same subset of \textsc{long} tasks of this
type, with the hardest (``put both the alphabet soup and the cream cheese box in the basket'')
below $16\%$ for every method: the policy typically places the first object and fails to
sequence the second before timeout (Fig.~\ref{fig:dualobj}). Single-subgoal \textsc{long}
tasks reach $70$--$80\%$, comparable to the other suites, so the low \textsc{long} averages
reflect error compounding across subgoals rather than a deficiency of any particular
post-training method.

Second, co-training helps most where the rollout data cannot: on skills the base policy never
executes successfully, its rollouts contain no positive examples, so rollout post-training
has nothing to reinforce. Drawer manipulation is the clearest case: the base policy rarely
opens a drawer, and only the co-trained policy acquires the skill reliably
(Fig.~\ref{fig:drawer}). Rollout data can only sharpen behavior the policy already shows;
skills it lacks entirely have to come from the demonstrations.

\subsection{SnapFlow: one-step inference}\label{sec:snapflow}
We distill the best fine-tuned policy with SnapFlow into a single-step generator and measure
inference on the class of hardware this work targets: an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano ($8$\,GB
unified memory), running the policy in bfloat16 (setup in Appendix~\ref{app:compute}).
Generating one $20$-action chunk takes $922\pm1$\,ms with the ten-step flow-matching solve and
$255\pm1$\,ms with SnapFlow's single step---a $3.6\times$ speed-up that raises the
re-planning rate from $1.1$ to $3.9$ chunks per second ($12.7$\,ms per action amortized over
the chunk). The remaining $255$\,ms is dominated by the vision-language prefix, which both

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
Our results support the transfer we set out to test: the RECAP recipe also improves a compact
VLA. The advantage-labeling threshold should be set permissively---an aggressive threshold
labels necessary actions negative and the policy partially unlearns them. Rollout-only
fine-tuning is likewise not uniformly safe: it degraded the long-horizon suite, and rollouts
can only reinforce behavior the policy already exhibits. Mixing demonstration batches into the
fine-tune addressed both issues in our experiments. One possible
explanation for why co-training matters more here than in RECAP is model capacity: a compact
policy may not have the capacity to retain its pre-training behavior through a rollout-only
fine-tune, so the demonstration batches act as explicit replay against forgetting. The same
limit may explain why guidance contributes so little (Sec.~\ref{sec:dose}): a small model may
not represent clearly separated positive and negative conditionals, leaving CFG little to
amplify. Our experiments do not isolate either effect, and the pending co-training run at
$\pf{=}0.8$ will show whether the long-horizon regression is specific to rollout-only
training.

Limitations: all results are in simulation on LIBERO with a single training seed per
configuration; per-task numbers at $50$ trials are noisy, so we draw conclusions only from
suite-level aggregates and consistent cross-method patterns. Reporting the maximum over a
five-point guidance sweep carries an optimistic bias of one to two points for the
conditioned methods; the unconditioned no-KI baseline gets no such benefit, so its gap is,
if anything, slightly overstated. We tested positive fractions $0.3$, $0.4$, and $0.8$
only. Latency was measured on-device but not in
closed-loop control, and real-robot validation of the post-training gains remains future
work.

\section{Conclusion}
Critic-guided rollout post-training transfers from large VLAs to SmolVLA: pre-training with
knowledge insulation and advantage conditioning improves over plain SmolVLA, and fine-tuning
on critic-labeled rollouts mixed with expert demonstrations yields our best policy on every
suite. Combined with one-step SnapFlow distillation
for real-time inference, this makes rollout-based improvement of VLA policies a practical
option for the compact models that edge and mobile robots can actually run.

\label{app:compute}
Fine-tunes run $20$k steps ($\approx90$~min on one NVIDIA H200). Evaluation is
simulation-bound rather than GPU-bound: a full $n{=}500$ suite evaluation takes
$\approx2.5$~h (\textsc{spatial}/\textsc{goal}) to $\approx6$~h (\textsc{long}) on an H200,
$\approx1.5$--$1.8\times$ longer on an RTX~A4500, and $\approx3.2\times$ longer on an
RTX~2080. Evaluations were therefore run on the smaller GPUs, reserving the H200 nodes for
training.

\textbf{Edge latency measurement.} The latencies in Sec.~\ref{sec:snapflow} were measured on
an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano ($8$\,GB unified memory, JetPack~6) with PyTorch~2.11, policy in
bfloat16 under autocast, batch size~$1$, two $256{\times}256$ cameras, and $48$ language
tokens: $30$ timed chunk generations after $8$ warm-up iterations (std $\approx1$\,ms). The
policy is the deployed architecture with random weights, which leaves latency unchanged; in
float32 the $453$M-parameter model exceeds the Orin Nano's memory, so bfloat16 is also the
practical deployment dtype.
